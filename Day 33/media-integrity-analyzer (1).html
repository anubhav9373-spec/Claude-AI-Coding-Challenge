Before you start

Two habits misinformation exploits

Concept

Headline Exaggeration

What it is: a headline that overstates, oversimplifies, or reframes a mundane finding as shocking, certain, or urgent.

Why it matters: most people only read the headline. If it misleads, the false impression sticks even if the article underneath is careful and accurate.

Where you meet it daily: news app push alerts, YouTube thumbnails, "you won't believe" link previews, health and finance clickbait.

How misinformation uses it: absolute words ("proves," "destroys," "everyone"), missing context ("studies show" — which study, how big?), and manufactured urgency ("before it's too late").

Concept

Emotional Manipulation

What it is: content engineered to trigger a strong feeling — fear, outrage, tribal loyalty — because strong feelings spread faster than calm analysis.

Why it matters: a racing pulse narrows attention. You're less likely to check a source when you're already reacting.

Where you meet it daily: social feeds, comment sections, influencer captions, breaking-news chyrons.

How misinformation uses it: loaded language, in-group/out-group framing ("they don't want you to know"), and artificial deadlines that discourage pausing to verify.

Next concept

How viral content finds your feelings

Concept

Targeted Emotional Triggers

What it is: specific words and framings chosen because they reliably provoke fear, anger, hope, or urgency in a particular audience.

Why it matters: content that makes you feel something gets shared more than content that makes you think something. Creators and bad actors both know this.

Where you meet it daily: comment sections, influencer captions, fundraising appeals, political posts.

How misinformation uses it: it names an enemy, promises insider knowledge, and asks you to act (share, buy, vote, panic) before you've had time to verify.
